If there's a correlation between two variables, the way to confirm that there is causality can be done by performing a series of experiments including those variables?

Yes, that's correct. To establish causality (rather than just correlation), you generally need to conduct controlled experiments. In these experiments, you manipulate one variable (often called the independent variable) and observe changes in the other variable (the dependent variable), controlling for other potential confounding factors. This approach helps to establish a cause-and-effect relationship by isolating the effect of the independent variable on the dependent one.

A few common experimental designs for determining causality include:
 
1. **Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs):**  Randomly assigning participants to either a treatment or control group, which helps to eliminate bias and confounding variables.
 
2. **Controlled Experiments:**  Manipulating one variable while holding all others constant to see if there’s an effect on the outcome variable.
 
3. **Natural Experiments:**  Observing naturally occurring situations where groups are subject to different conditions, though this is less controlled and can have limitations.
 
4. **Quasi-experiments:**  These involve manipulation but lack random assignment, which can still provide evidence for causality, albeit less rigorously than RCTs.

Without experiments, causality cannot be confidently established, as correlation alone does not imply causation due to potential confounding variables or reverse causality.
